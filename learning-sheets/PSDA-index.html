Topic 1

Ratios, rates and percentages

1.1

Ratios, rates and proportions

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Write and simplify part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios, and use a ratio to find the size of each part.
  2. Set up and solve proportions, including ratio tables, scale drawings and maps.
  3. Calculate unit rates and use them to compare rates, such as best buys and speeds, and to solve rate problems.
  4. Identify proportional relationships y = kx from tables, graphs, equations and descriptions, and interpret the constant k.
  5. Solve problems in which a ratio changes after quantities are added, removed or moved.

1.2

Units and unit conversion

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Convert a quantity from one unit to another by multiplying by conversion factors, including chains of two or more factors.
  2. Convert rates such as kilometers per hour to meters per second by converting the units in both the numerator and the denominator.
  3. Solve problems involving rates with units, such as cost per item, flow rate and fuel use, by using amount = rate × time or quantity.
  4. Use density (mass per unit volume) and population density (people per unit area) to find a missing mass, volume, population or area.
  5. Convert square and cubic units, and interpret the units of a quantity, coefficient or expression in context.

1.3

Percentages

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Calculate a percent of a quantity, and determine what percent one quantity is of another, including values read from tables and graphs.
  2. Calculate percent increase and percent decrease, and apply a single percent change with a multiplier in contexts such as discounts, tax and markups.
  3. Apply successive percent changes by multiplying their multipliers, including repeated changes and interest.
  4. Determine an original amount from a value after one or more percent changes.
  5. Distinguish percentage points from percent change, and solve concentration and mixture problems that use percentages.

Topic 2

Data analysis

2.1

Center and spread of one-variable data

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Determine the mean, median, mode and range of a data set given as a list or a graph.
  2. Use the relationship sum = mean × number of values to find a missing value, a total or an unknown in a data set.
  3. Determine a weighted mean, including the mean of two or more combined groups.
  4. Determine how adding, removing or changing a value, including an outlier, affects the mean, median and range.
  5. Compare standard deviations qualitatively and choose the mean or the median as the better measure of center for skewed data.

2.2

Data displays and comparing distributions

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Read and interpret bar graphs, dot plots, histograms and frequency tables to find counts, totals and percents.
  2. Calculate the mean and median of data given in a frequency table or dot plot, and identify the interval of a histogram that contains the median.
  3. Determine the five-number summary, range and interquartile range from a box plot, and interpret the quarters of the data it shows.
  4. Compare two distributions by their centers (mean, median) and spreads (range, interquartile range, standard deviation).
  5. Identify the shape of a distribution as symmetric, skewed left or skewed right, and determine how the shape affects the relationship between the mean and the median.

2.3

Two-variable data and scatterplots

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Read and interpret scatterplots, line graphs and tables of two variables to find values, counts, changes and rates of change.
  2. Describe the association between two variables as positive, negative or none, and as linear or nonlinear and strong or weak.
  3. Determine the equation of a line of best fit and interpret its slope and y-intercept in context.
  4. Use a line of best fit to make predictions and compare predicted values with actual values (residuals).
  5. Choose between a linear and an exponential model for data by testing for constant differences or constant ratios.

Topic 3

Probability and statistical inference

3.1

Probability and conditional probability

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Calculate the probability of an event as favorable outcomes divided by total outcomes, and use the complement rule P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  2. Determine probabilities from a two-way table, including "and," "or" and "neither" events, using the grand total as the denominator and filling in missing cells.
  3. Determine conditional probabilities ("given that," "of those who") by restricting the denominator to the correct row or column.
  4. Calculate expected counts from a probability or a sample proportion, and work backward from a count to a probability or a total.
  5. Identify whether two events are independent by comparing conditional probabilities, and use the multiplication rule for independent events.

3.2

Sampling, inference and margin of error

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Identify the population, sample, sample statistic and population parameter described in a study.
  2. Use a sample proportion or sample mean to estimate a population proportion, mean or total.
  3. Construct and interpret an interval estimate of the form estimate ± margin of error, and recover the estimate and margin of error from an interval.
  4. Determine how sample size and variability in the data affect the margin of error.
  5. Determine which conclusions a margin of error supports, including when two estimates have overlapping intervals.

3.3

Evaluating statistical claims

5 learning sheets · 12 guided questions · 2 quiz sheets × 20

Learning objectives
  1. Identify random sampling methods, and explain how convenience samples, voluntary response samples and nonresponse can bias results.
  2. Determine the largest population to which a sample result can be generalized, and use a random sample to make estimates only about the population that was sampled.
  3. Distinguish observational studies from experiments by deciding whether the researchers imposed the treatment.
  4. Determine when a study supports a cause-and-effect conclusion, recognizing the role of random assignment and of confounding variables.
  5. Identify the most appropriate conclusion from a study description by checking both how subjects were selected and how treatments were assigned.
